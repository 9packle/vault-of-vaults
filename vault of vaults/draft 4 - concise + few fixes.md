The flame tests I initially encountered were compelling to me because I wanted to know why a red flame would be produced by the presence of lithium, whereas a blue-green one would be produced by the presence of copper. In physics I learnt that certain wavelengths of light would be released when electrons dropped from one discrete level to another, given by ΔE = hc/λ, and these levels would be different for different elements as they have varying amounts of protons in the nuclei. This provided me a much clearer chain of reasoning for the colours of the flames, and also showed me how Physics and Maths can be the tools which give predictive power to chemistry, which drew me towards studying the principles which lie between chemistry and physics.
My subject choices of Maths, Further Maths, Physics and Chemistry also tie into my achievements in competitions, which I actively looked for as a way to stretch my understanding of the subjects during both the paper and the preparation process leading up to the competition. Preparation leading to my two merits in the BMO required me to learn how to rigorously structure solutions while ruling out all other possibilities. This way of thinking also aided me for some organic synthesis questions where I initially wasn't able to work out the next molecule in the step, as I could use a similar way of thinking to eliminate incorrect answers with the given information.
My interest in Chemistry led me to seek further challenges in the UKChO and C3L6 in order to hone my understanding of underlying principles and explore new territory. Throughout the preparation I was exposed to more complicated organic synthesis questions, which were my favourite questions since initially I felt challenged by the unfamiliar questions, but I slowly learnt common reagents such as Grignard reagents, which could more easily add longer carbon chains using the organometallic reagents' nucleophilicity, and types of reactions like the Wolff-Kishner reduction to be able to work through each step of synthesis pathways. Each piece of information I picked up felt rewarding since often through looking at one type of mechanism, many other scenarios with similar functional groups could also be solved by using the same information. Later on as I was able to work out more and more structures in a question, I felt a sense of satisfaction seeing my efforts and interests come to fruition, finally resulting in a Gold in the Olympiad and two in C3L6, which felt like both recognition and concrete proof for the increase in my knowledge as well as a sign of encouragement to continue to look at new topics.
Although A level Chemistry gave me the basic tools, I was often left desiring a better theory which could better predict certain phenomena rather than only giving a description. One such phenomena was how the diatomic oxygen molecule was attracted to the magnet, when by its Lewis structure there should have been no unpaired electrons which would lead to this behaviour. Reading about MO theory in Keeler and Wothers' "why chemical reactions happen" showed me how this theory predicts the behaviour of the molecule, as there would be one electron in each of the two degenerate π* orbitals, leading naturally to the paramagnetism exhibited by oxygen molecules. I also looked at another question where I had to distinguish between libethenite and turquoise, which taught me the importance of reasoning with ambiguous data as well as the gap between theory and experimentation, since their percentages of copper by mass fell in between the theoretical numbers, requiring me to make a judgement while understanding that such differences might arise from mixtures or impurities. The difference between theory and experimentation came up again when synthesising ethyl ethanoate, as my yield was far lower than the expected amount. This along with the smell of vinegar and the wide range of the sample's boiling point both reflected the nature of the reversible equilibrium as well as possible steps I could have taken, such as continuously distilling off the ester so that the equilibrium shifts more towards the reactants so that more ester is produced as well as using sodium carbonate to neutralise the unreacted ethanoic acid which produces the smell of vinegar and purify the product.
My understanding of chemistry becomes more refined when I must review and explain my current understanding of a topic and accept more accurate theories and predictions when experimental evidence differs from the theory, and I want to study chemistry where mechanisms are understood through thermodynamics, quantum mechanics and mathematics.
