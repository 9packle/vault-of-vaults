I have always been interested in the sciences since starting formally, and I came to especially enjoy chemistry from all the interesting experiments and demonstrations. One such example would be the flame colour test for identifying metals. I wanted to know why the red flame would be produced by the presence of lithium, or a blue-green on by the presence of copper. The Bohr model suggested discrete energy levels where the electrons would be in orbitals, and in physics we learnt about how certain wavelengths of light would be released when electrons dropped from one discrete level to another, given by ΔE = hc/λ, and these levels would be different for different elements. This provided me a much clearer chain of reasoning for the colours emitted using the quantum-mechanical nature of the electrons' transitions, and also showed how physics and maths can be the tools which give predictive power to chemistry, which is where I want to study: in between the lines of just chemistry or physics.
My subject choices of Maths, Further Maths, Physics and Chemistry also tie into my achievements in competitions, which I actively looked for as a way to stretch my understanding of the subjects, both in the paper and in the preparation process for the competition during which I pushed past the level the school curriculum required of me. I achieved a Gold in the Senior Maths Challenge twice and subsequently a Merit in the British Maths Olympiad both times. The Olympiad was very different and required me to learn to rigorously structure solutions while ruling out all other possibilities so that by logic the answer provided was the only one. This way of thinking aided me for some organic synthesis questions where I initially wasn't able to work out the next molecule in the step, as I could use a similar way of thinking to eliminate possible answers with the given information until the answer revealed itself as the only possibility after rigorous inspection.
My interest in Chemistry led me to achieve a Gold in Round 1 of the UK Chemistry Olympiad as well as a Gold twice in C3L6. Throughout the preparation I was exposed to more complicated organic synthesis questions which were much more involved than organic chemistry at A level, which were my favourite questions since initially I savoured the challenge they posed while knowing very little of the common reagents and reaction pathways, but I slowly learnt common reagents such as Grignard reagents, and types of reactions like the Wolff-Kishner reduction to be able to work through each step of synthesis pathways. Each piece of information I picked up felt rewarding since often through looking at one type of mechanism, many other scenarios with similar functional groups could also be solved by using the same information. Later on as I was able to work out more and more structures in a question, I felt a sense of satisfaction seeing my efforts and interests come to fruition.
Although A level Chemistry gave me the basic tools, I was often left wanting of a better theory which could better predict certain phenomena rather than only giving a description. One such phenomena was how the diatomic oxygen molecule was attracted to the magnet, when by its Lewis structure there should have been no unpaired electrons which would lead to this behaviour. Reading about MO theory in Keeler and Wothers' "why chemical reactions happen" showed me how this theory predicts the behaviour of the molecule, as there would be one electron in each of the two degenerate π* orbitals, leading naturally to the paramagnetism exhibited by oxygen molecules. I also looked at other types of questions like the one in the chemistry olympiad where I had to distinguish between libethenite and turquoise, which taught me the importance of reasoning with ambiguous data as well as the gap between theory and experimentation, since their percentages of copper by mass fell in between the theoretical numbers, requiring me to make a judgement while understanding that such differences might arise from mixtures or impurities.
My understanding of chemistry becomes more refined when I must review and explain my current understanding of a topic, defend my thought process and accept more accurate theories and predictions. I want to study chemistry where mechanisms are understood through thermodynamics, quantum mechanics and mathematics and where small-group teaching forces me to constantly refine my reasoning.
