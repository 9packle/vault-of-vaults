My interest in chemistry increasingly drew me towards physical principles which govern molecular behaviour. I was often surprised by how physics underpinned the workings for several chemistry topics, such as the cell potentials from A level, or the viability of a reaction. While looking at A-level reactions such as esterification or dehydration of cyclohexanol, I often wondered what determined the viability of a reaction. This then led me to the concept Gibbs free energy, which combines entropy and enthalpy to provide a value which could be used to predict the thermodynamic viability of a reaction: if ΔG < 0, then the reaction would be spontaneous, but not necessarily observable on laboratory timescales. The same concept also ties in with the cell potentials, with ΔG = -nFE giving the maximum possible work done by the cell. This connection between physics and chemistry draws me towards the Natural Sciences course. I was also intrigued by a video on making a YBCO superconductor which involved both chemistry knowledge of how to accurately synthesise the material with the correct proportions and physics in how this chemical makeup would allow the phenomenon of superconductivity to occur. This led me to choose to research more on this topic when we were tasked in school to pick a physics topic of our choosing and present on it. I began with BCS theory as it was the first theory to provide a mechanism for the phenomenon of superconductivity. Reading about the explanation of how the formation of Cooper pairs exempts them from the Pauli exclusion principle and allows them to condense into a and their central role in providing a mechanism for superconductivity intrigued me, but further research showed me that this theory did not provide a valid mechanism for high-temperature superconductors such as the YBCO superconductor in the video.
