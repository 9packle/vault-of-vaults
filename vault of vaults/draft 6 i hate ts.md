The flame tests I initially encountered were compelling to me because I wanted to know why a red flame would be produced by the presence of lithium, whereas copper hydroxide would produce a blue-green flame. In physics I learnt that certain wavelengths of light would be released when electrons dropped from one discrete level to another, given by ΔE = hc/λ, with these levels being dependent on effective nuclear charge and electronic configuration. This provided a much clearer chain of reasoning for the colours of the flames, and also showed me how Physics and Maths can be the tools which give predictive power to chemistry. This convinced me that boundaries between the sciences can be seen as artificial, and sometimes the most interesting questions lie between.

My subject choices tie into my achievements in competitions, which I actively sought out as a way to stretch my understanding of the subjects during both the preparation process leading up to the competition. Preparation leading to my two merits in the BMO required me to learn how to rigorously structure solutions while ruling out all other possibilities, which also aided me for organic synthesis questions, as I could use a similar way of thinking to eliminate incorrect structures. I also sought further challenges in the UKChO and C3L6 in order to hone my understanding of underlying principles and explore new territory. Throughout the preparation I was exposed to more complicated organic synthesis questions, which were my favourite since I felt challenged by the unfamiliar questions, but afterwards I slowly learnt common reagents and reaction mechanisms such as Grignard reagents, which can add longer carbon chains using the high nucleophilicity of the carbon adjacent to the metal to react with the electrophilic carbon of a carbonyl group, or the Wolff-Kishner reduction, which can be used to convert carbonyl groups to methylene groups through converting the carbonyl to a hydrazone, then removing the group through deprotonating the nitrogen not bonded to the carbon, I was able to work through each step of synthesis pathways. 

Although A level Chemistry gave me the basic tools, I was often left desiring theories which could better predict certain phenomena rather than giving a description. One such phenomenon was how oxygen molecules were attracted to a magnet, since by its Lewis structure there should have been no unpaired electrons which would lead to this behaviour. Reading about MO theory in Keeler and Wothers' "Why Chemical Reactions Happen" showed me how this theory predicts the behaviour of the molecule, as there would be one electron in each of the two degenerate π* orbitals by Hund's rule, leading naturally to the paramagnetism exhibited by oxygen molecules. I also looked at another question where I had to distinguish between libethenite and turquoise, which taught me the importance of reasoning with ambiguous data as well as the gap between theory and experimentation, since their percentages of copper by mass fell in between the theoretical numbers, requiring me to make a judgement while understanding that such differences might arise from mixtures or impurities. The difference between theory and experimentation re-emerged when synthesising ethyl ethanoate, as my yield was far lower than the expected amount. The smell of vinegar and the wide range of the sample's boiling point both reflected the nature of the reversible equilibrium as well as possible steps I could have taken, such as continuously distilling off the ester so that the equilibrium shifts more towards the products so that more ester is produced. I could have also used sodium carbonate to neutralise the unreacted ethanoic acid responsible for the smell of vinegar and to purify the product. This has sharpened my ability to recognise the gap between theory and experimentation, which I hope to develop more at university.
