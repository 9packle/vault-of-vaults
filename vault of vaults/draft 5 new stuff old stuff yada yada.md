The flame tests I initially encountered were compelling to me because I wanted to know why a red flame would be produced by the presence of lithium, whereas a blue-green one would be produced by the presence of copper. In physics I learnt that certain wavelengths of light would be released when electrons dropped from one discrete level to another, given by ΔE = hc/λ, with these levels being dependent on factors such as effective nuclear charge and electronic configuration. This provided a much clearer chain of reasoning for the colours of the flames, and also showed me how Physics and Maths can be the tools which give predictive power to chemistry. This convinced me that boundaries between the sciences can be seen as artificial, and sometimes the most interesting questions lie between.

Although A level Chemistry gave me the basic tools, I was often left desiring theories which could better predict certain phenomena rather than giving a description. One such phenomena was how oxygen molecules were attracted to a magnet, when by its Lewis structure there should have been no unpaired electrons which would lead to this behaviour. Reading about MO theory in Keeler and Wothers' "Why Chemical Reactions Happen" showed me how this theory predicts the behaviour of the molecule, as there would be one electron in each of the two degenerate π* orbitals by Hund's rule, leading naturally to the paramagnetism exhibited by oxygen molecules. I also looked at another question where I had to distinguish between libethenite and turquoise, which taught me the importance of reasoning with ambiguous data as well as the gap between theory and experimentation, since their percentages of copper by mass fell in between the theoretical numbers, requiring me to make a judgement while understanding that such differences might arise from mixtures or impurities. The difference between theory and experimentation came up again when synthesising ethyl ethanoate, as my yield was far lower than the expected amount. This, along with the smell of vinegar and the wide range of the sample's boiling point both reflected the nature of the reversible equilibrium as well as possible steps I could have taken, such as continuously distilling off the ester so that the equilibrium shifts more towards the products so that more ester is produced as well as using sodium carbonate to neutralise the unreacted ethanoic acid which produces the smell of vinegar and purify the product. This has sharpened my ability to recognise the gap between theory and experimentation, which I hope to develop more at university.
