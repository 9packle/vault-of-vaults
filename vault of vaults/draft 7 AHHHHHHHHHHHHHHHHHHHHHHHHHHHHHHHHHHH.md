When measuring the enthalpy of neutralisation in a calorimetry practical, my values were consistently less exothermic than the literature value. The textbook attributed this to heat loss, yet the experience left me dissatisfied: even with minimal experimental error, the tabulated bond-energy calculations still deviated from measured enthalpies. I found a satisfying answer during a UKChO problem: bond energy is a statistical average across molecular environments and not a fixed property of any single bond. That moment changed how I saw chemistry: not as a catalogue of fixed truths, but as a discipline whose predictive power depends on the models we choose. This drew me towards chemistry's overlap with physics and mathematics. Flame tests offered a visual example: lithium's red and copper's blue-green emissions arise from electrons transitioning between discrete energy levels, with wavelengths given by ΔE = hc/λ. I came to see disciplinary boundaries as artificial. I want to study chemistry at the intersection of mathematical modelling and empirical observation, deriving macroscopic behaviour from fundamental principles.

My subject choices helped me to develop quantitative rigour and intuition and taught me to ask questions at the molecular level. This perspective was tested during an ethyl ethanoate esterification. A low yield, the smell of vinegar, and broad boiling range confirmed incomplete conversion and distilling off the ester or neutralising the acid could have driven the equilibrium forward. The experience showed me how the gap between prediction and observation can serve as diagnostic tools. Preparation leading to my two merits in the BMO required me to learn how to rigorously structure solutions while ruling out all other possibilities, which also aided me for organic synthesis questions, as I could use a similar way of thinking to eliminate incorrect structures. I also sought further challenges in the UKChO and C3L6 in order to hone my understanding of underlying principles and explore new territory. Throughout the preparation I was exposed to more complicated organic synthesis questions, which were my favourite since I felt challenged by the unfamiliar questions. I slowly learnt common reagents and reaction mechanisms; recognising when a Grignard reagent could extend a carbon chain or when a Wolff-Kishner would remove a carbonyl.

Although A level Chemistry gave me the basic tools, I was often left desiring theories which could better predict certain phenomena rather than giving a description. One such phenomenon was how oxygen molecules were attracted to a magnet, since by its Lewis structure there should have been no unpaired electrons which would lead to this behaviour. Reading about MO theory in Keeler and Wothers' "Why Chemical Reactions Happen" showed me how this theory predicts the behaviour and structure of the molecule, as there would be one electron in each of the two degenerate π* orbitals by Hund's rule, leading naturally to the paramagnetism exhibited by oxygen molecules, while the resulting bond order from the bonding and anti-bonding orbitals matched the double bond also described by its Lewis structure. Another question where I had to distinguish libethenite from turquoise taught the lesson in reverse: when the copper percentage fell between the two ideal formulae, the honest conclusion was mixture or impurity, not a forced choice. Natural samples refuse ideal formulae, and theories likewise refuse exactness. The ideal gas law holds only while molecular size and attraction stay negligible; real gases under pressure mark where that neglect fails. Together these studies trained me not to collect results but to interrogate them, to ask what a deviation reveals about the model behind it.
